Bacteriophage Therapy

Highest Upside · Pursue Multiple Programs in Parallel

Bacteriophages penetrate biofilm on prosthetic material — a property that antibiotics fundamentally lack. There is a published IV phage case in exactly this scenario (mechanical AVR PVE + paravalvular root abscess, non-operative candidate) that achieved complete recovery. Belgian and French compassionate registries now report ~75–80% infection control at 3 months when phage is added to antibiotics for prosthesis-associated S. aureus infections. Contact all relevant programs simultaneously — phage strain matching takes time and parallel outreach is strongly recommended.

Direct precedent: Gilbey et al. (Med J Aust, 2019) — first IV phage (AB-SA01, three-phage Myoviridae cocktail) for a patient with prosthetic AVR endocarditis + native mitral involvement + paravalvular root abscess deemed non-operative. Given 2×/day IV for 14 days alongside antibiotics. Complete recovery, no adverse events.

Endovascular Intervention — Pseudoaneurysm Exclusion

Urgent · Time-Sensitive

Covered Endovascular Stent-Graft — Percutaneous Pseudoaneurysm Exclusion

Rupture Risk = Most Likely Lethal Event Bridge or Definitive Palliation Discuss Urgently

The perfused pseudoaneurysm adjacent to the valve/abscess complex represents the most immediate mechanical threat in this case. While surgical correction remains the gold standard, a covered endovascular stent-graft (ascending-aorta cuff / TEVAR-style) can be deployed percutaneously to exclude the pseudoaneurysm lumen — eliminating rupture risk without open surgery.

Published precedent: Case reports document successful percutaneous exclusion of mycotic ascending aortic pseudoaneurysms using covered stent-grafts in combination with prolonged antibiotics in MSSA patients. The approach has been used as both a bridge to definitive surgery and as definitive palliation in non-operative candidates.
  • Mechanism: Covered stent-graft deployed via femoral artery access, excluding the pseudoaneurysm sac from the aortic lumen — eliminates pulsatile pressure on the infected wall
  • Does not cure the underlying infection — must be combined with ongoing antimicrobial therapy and/or phage therapy
  • Technical consideration: The proximity to the aortic valve and prosthetic material requires precise sizing and positioning — only experienced aortic centers should attempt this
  • Who to contact immediately: Cleveland Aorta Center (Cleveland Clinic), Hannover MHH Vascular Surgery, West German Heart and Vascular Center Essen, Baylor/Texas Heart Institute (Coselli group)

Salvage & Novel Antimicrobial Strategies

Evidence-Based · Biofilm-Targeted

Standard-of-care antibiotics have been tried. The following combinations target biofilm-producing S. aureus on prosthetic material specifically. All require knowledge of current MICs. The goal is a "biofilm-tuned" regimen combining a cell-wall active agent + daptomycin + a biofilm penetrator, with long-term dalbavancin suppression once infection is biologically controlled.

High-Dose Daptomycin + Ceftaroline/Ceftobiprole ± Fosfomycin — The Biofilm Triple

Strongest Case Evidence Anti-Biofilm Available Now

The most evidence-supported salvage regimen for refractory S. aureus PVE. Synergy between daptomycin and ceftaroline/ceftobiprole is well-characterized — the beta-lactam restores daptomycin susceptibility by modifying cell membrane phospholipid composition, achieving bactericidal activity even against daptomycin-non-susceptible strains. Adding fosfomycin IV (the "BACSARM-style" triple) provides additional biofilm penetration.

Key evidence: Published case: successful treatment of MRSA aortic PVE with prolonged high-dose daptomycin + ceftaroline when vancomycin failed (PMID 26058777). Synergistic checkerboard assays confirmed for daptomycin + ceftaroline + fosfomycin against high-inoculum daptomycin-non-susceptible MRSA — directly applicable to biofilm-embedded prosthetic infection.
  • Daptomycin: 8–10 mg/kg/day IV — high dose is critical; standard doses underpenetrate vegetations
  • Ceftobiprole (Zevtera): 500 mg IV q8h — FDA approved 2023 for S. aureus bacteremia/endocarditis; NEJM Phase 3 RCT showed non-inferiority to daptomycin across complicated bacteremia spectrum
  • Ceftaroline: 600 mg IV q8h — alternative if ceftobiprole unavailable; established synergy data
  • Fosfomycin IV: 8g IV q6–8h or continuous infusion — available in Germany/EU without special access; biofilm penetration mechanism distinct from all other agents
  • Rifampin: 300–450 mg q8–12h if susceptible — add only after blood cultures are confirmed negative (prevents resistance selection); unique activity against stationary-phase organisms in biofilm

Dalbavancin — Long-Term Outpatient Suppression Backbone

Suppressive Strategy 4–9 Week Dosing Intervals (TDM-guided)

Dalbavancin's 14-day half-life enables TDM-guided dosing intervals of 4–9 weeks on an outpatient basis — dramatically reducing hospitalization burden while maintaining therapeutic levels. Published case reports document long-term use in inoperable PVE maintaining hemodynamic stability. Once the infection is biologically controlled (negative cultures, stable CRP), dalbavancin becomes the practical suppression backbone.

  • 1500 mg IV loading dose, then 1000 mg at TDM-guided intervals (4–9 weeks)
  • Covers MRSA and MSSA; excellent cardiac tissue penetration
  • 2024 systematic review (Springer Infection) confirms use in IE sequential therapy
  • Goal: sustained bactericidal coverage preventing relapse, reducing PICC/central line complications from daily IV antibiotics

Palliative Framework — When Curative Intent Is Not Possible

Long-Term Stability

Suppressive Antibiotic Therapy (SAT) — 2025 Evidence

SAT May Outperform Conventional Antibiotics Palliative

A 2025 European Heart Journal Supplements study and a separate April 2025 ScienceDirect retrospective both found that structured SAT was associated with better outcomes than conventional antibiotic therapy (CAT) in non-operated PVE despite surgical indication. SAT is not "stopping antibiotics" — it is a formal, ongoing, closely monitored clinical strategy designed to suppress infection below the threshold of sepsis and embolic risk.

  • Oral suppressive options: TMP-SMX, clindamycin, or rifampin-based (depending on sensitivities)
  • IV outpatient suppression: Dalbavancin weekly — most practical for chronic management
  • Serial imaging: TEE/CT to monitor pseudoaneurysm size and abscess progression
  • Dynamic reassessment: Pseudoaneurysm enlargement changes the risk-benefit of intervention — ongoing monitoring is essential
  • Adjunctive: IVIG in toxin-mediated cases; HBOT has theoretical benefit for ischemic tissue oxygen delivery but limited cardiac infection data
